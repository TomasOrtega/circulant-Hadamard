# Video 1.1 — What is the group ring?

Goal: define $\mathbb Z[C_n]$ and prove every element has a unique representative of degree less than $n$.

## Why we need a new kind of polynomial

We want to describe a row whose positions form a cycle. If a sign moves right past the last position, it should come back to the first.

Ordinary polynomials don't do that automatically. If we multiply a polynomial by $X$, its highest power just gets larger. So let's change the rules to match our circle.

Take the cyclic group $C_n$, generated by a symbol $X$, with the rule $X^n=1$. The elements of this group are

$$
1,X,X^2,\ldots,X^{n-1}.
$$

The symbol $X$ is not a number we're substituting. For now it simply records one step around the cycle.

*[Animation: Draw $n$ positions around a circle, labeled $1,X,\ldots,X^{n-1}$. Move a pointer around and show $X^n$ returning to the same position as $1$.]*

## Allow integer coefficients

A group ring lets us attach an integer to each group element and add them. An element looks like

$$
a_0+a_1X+\cdots+a_{n-1}X^{n-1},\qquad a_j\in\mathbb Z.
$$

We add coefficients in the usual way, and we multiply using the rule $X^n=1$.

This is exactly the quotient ring

$$
\mathbb Z[C_n]\cong\mathbb Z[X]/(X^n-1).
$$

The quotient notation means that we treat $X^n-1$ as zero. Every time an exponent reaches $n$, we can subtract $n$ from that exponent.

For $n=4$, for example,

$$
X^4=1,\qquad X^5=X,\qquad X^{-1}=X^3.
$$

*[Animation: Start with a term $3X^5$; rotate its marker once around the four slots and rewrite it as $3X$. Repeat with $X^{-1}$ moving one position backward.]*

## Why degree less than n is enough

Every polynomial can be reduced to a polynomial of degree less than $n$: replace $X^n$ by $1$, $X^{n+1}$ by $X$, and so on.

And this reduced expression is unique. Indeed, if two polynomials of degree less than $n$ represented the same element, their difference would be a multiple of $X^n-1$. But a nonzero multiple of $X^n-1$ cannot have degree less than $n$.

So our coefficients are unambiguous. The ordered list $(a_0,\ldots,a_{n-1})$ is exactly the same information as the ring element $a_0+\cdots+a_{n-1}X^{n-1}$.

*[Animation: Show two descriptions connected by a two-way arrow: an $n$-slot coefficient strip and a polynomial of degree less than $n$.]*

This is the group ring. Next, we will see why multiplying by $X$ is precisely the operation of shifting a row.
