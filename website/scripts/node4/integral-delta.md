# Video 4.4 — Why the Alternating Product Is Integral

Goal: show that the quotient defining $\Delta(f)$ has no denominator
obstruction at any prime, so it is an algebraic integer.

## Slide 1 — Pair the same product in one direction

Let $I$ be the distinct primes dividing $u$. All our evaluated values
belong to $B=\mathbb Z[i,\{\zeta_p:p\in I\}]$, the ring generated by
the roots we are using.

Fix $p\in I$. Pair each subset not containing $p$ with the subset
obtained by adding it:

$$
\Delta(f)=\prod_{\substack{S\subseteq I\\p\notin S}}
\left(\frac{f(\zeta_S)}{f(\zeta_{S\cup\{p\}})}\right)^{(-1)^{|S|}}.
$$

This is the same alternating product, regrouped into comparisons in
the $p$-direction.

*[Animation: Use the four cards for two primes $p,q$. First show
$f(1)f(\zeta_p\zeta_q)/(f(\zeta_p)f(\zeta_q))$, then group it as
$[f(1)/f(\zeta_p)]/[f(\zeta_q)/f(\zeta_p\zeta_q)]$.
Move cards without changing their numerator or denominator roles.]*

## Slide 2 — Check the primes dividing u

At every prime above $p$, Video 4.3 makes each paired ratio a unit with
residue one. Products and inverses preserve both properties, so the same
is true of $\Delta(f)$.

Now change the pairing direction and repeat for every prime dividing
$u$. We have checked the same number at every one of these primes.

*[Animation: Mark each ratio in the $p$-pairing “unit, residue $1$,” then
collapse the labels onto $\Delta(f)$. Regroup the four cards in the
$q$-direction and repeat. Maintain one fixed $\Delta(f)$ label throughout;
we are changing the grouping, not defining a new product.]*

## Slide 3 — Check all the other primes

At a prime not dividing $u$, we are allowed to divide by $u^2$.
The norm identity gives the explicit inverse

$$
f(\zeta_S)^{-1}=\frac{\overline{f(\zeta_S)}}{u^2}.
$$

Every evaluated factor is therefore a unit at that prime. So
$\Delta(f)$ has no denominator obstruction there either.

*[Animation: Split a checklist into “primes dividing $u$” and “all other
primes.” Keep the first side checked by the paired ratios. On the second,
multiply each value by its displayed inverse to obtain $1$, then check
the whole product.]*

## Slide 4 — The local checks give a global answer

Why do these checks imply $\Delta(f)\in B$? If a fraction were outside
$B$, the elements of $B$ that clear its denominator would form a proper
ideal. Choose a maximal ideal containing them. A representation with a
denominator outside that ideal would itself clear the fraction, which
is a contradiction. Thus a fraction belonging to every local ring must
already belong to $B$.

We have proved

$$
\boxed{\Delta(f)\in B.}
$$

Roots of unity are algebraic integers, and sums and products of algebraic
integers are algebraic integers. Hence $\Delta(f)$ is one too. Combined
with the unit-circle condition, this lets us apply Kronecker's theorem.
Keep the residue-one information: we will also need it to restrict the order.

*[Animation: Draw a proposed denominator obstruction inside one maximal
ideal. Show that any allowed local denominator would both lie outside
that ideal and belong to the set of clearing multipliers inside it.
Cross out the obstruction, then bring together “algebraic integer” and
“all conjugates have magnitude $1$.” Keep “residue $1$ above $p\mid u$”
as a separate result to carry forward.]*
